## Two degrees, one question

I studied Law and Economics at the same time, in Maceió. The combination was not a career plan: it was the realisation that the questions I cared about — why a rule produces one outcome rather than another, how much a public decision costs, who wins and who loses from it — did not fit entirely inside either field. I practised law between 2010 and 2014, mostly in tax and administrative matters, and during that period completed a master's degree in Applied Economics at UFAL, studying the location of industry in Northeast Brazil.

## University and the justice system

In 2014 I joined the Alagoas State Court of Justice (TJAL) as a Judicial Analyst in Economics. There I have written technical opinions, budget-impact estimates and expert analyses in administrative proceedings and, since 2023, I advise trial judges. Work at the court is a constant source of research questions: how the value of a damages award is formed, why similar cases take such different amounts of time, what changes when a court adopts an artificial-intelligence tool.

In parallel, I completed a PhD in Economics at UFPB, with a thesis on information and communication technology in the economics of education, and in 2023 I became a tenured professor at UFAL, Penedo Educational Unit, in the Information Systems programme. Before that, between 2019 and 2021, I was a substitute lecturer in the Tourism programme of the same unit.

## A foot in the world of public service examinations

Both of my current positions came through competitive public examinations (concursos), which are part of my trajectory both as a candidate and as a civil servant. I passed eight selections for economist, professor, judicial analyst and municipal attorney positions — three of them in first place, including the tenured professorship at UFAL and the economist post at CBTU — and I was certified in the National Judiciary Examination (ENAM). I still sit public examinations and follow calls, examination boards and the structuring of public careers, which keeps alive my interest in evaluation, management and personnel policy in the public sector. The full list is in the [CV](/en/cv/#exams-title).

## The turn towards Information Systems

Teaching future technology professionals changed how I do research. I began treating public data as infrastructure — something that needs reproducible pipelines, documentation and version control — and designing projects that produce, beyond the paper, a dashboard, an open dataset or a tool someone can use. R, Python, SQL and spatial analysis stopped being merely estimation tools and became part of the object of study: how information systems transform public administration and the judiciary.

## From the classroom to new fronts

Undergraduate courses opened fronts I had not planned. Working on Tourism topics in Penedo — a historic town on the banks of the São Francisco river — sparked an interest in international tourism demand and territorial development. Teaching Fisheries Economics to Fisheries Engineering students in 2026 led to a research programme on marine heatwaves, seasonal closures and marine protected areas, using vessel-tracking data. And teaching subjects that connect Law, Economics and technology feeds the projects on jurimetrics, artificial intelligence and digital government.

## What guides the work

Three commitments run through everything I do: use public data whenever possible, paying attention to its coverage and its flaws; document the method so that someone else can reproduce the result; and return what research produces — as a lecture, a dashboard, a bulletin or an expert opinion — to those who have to decide. I also write weekly on [LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/in/valber-gregory-49013744) about these areas — applied economics, jurimetrics, AI in the public sector, tourism, ports and fisheries — in short pieces written for non-specialists. Many of the projects described on this site are ongoing. Results may change after further validation, and the site is updated to reflect that.
